0.q Explain the octet rule
0.a The tendency of atoms to achieve the noble gas configuration and surround themselves with 8 electrons. The number of missing electrons is "borrowed" from a bonding atom by sharing them; this number gives the number of bonds.
1.q Which exceptions are there to the octet rule?
1.a For helium, He, and hydrogen, H, the duet rule applies: in H<sub>2</sub> each H atom contributes 1 of its valence electrons. He surrounds itself with 2 of its own valence electrons. For the transition metals the 18-electron rule applies.
2.q Name 6 atoms for which the octet rule holds
3.q Which atom sometimes has a different number of bonds from what the octet rule predicts?
4.q Explain what the octet rule means for carbon
4.a Carbon, C, has 4 valence electrons. By forming 4 covalent bonds, C gets 8 electrons around it, for example in CH<sub>4</sub>
5.q Explain what the octet rule means for chlorine
5.a Chlorine, Cl, has 7 valence electrons. By forming 1 covalent bond, Cl gets 8 electrons around it, for example in Cl<sub>2</sub>
6.q How do you show non-bonding valence electrons in a Lewis structure?
6.a With a dot
7.q How do you show bonding electrons in a Lewis structure?
7.a Two bonding electrons as a line; in a double bond 4 electrons are involved in the covalent bond, so 2 lines
8.q Set out the step-by-step plan for the Lewis structure of NH<sub>3</sub>
8.a Step 1: N has 5 valence electrons, H has 1, so 8 electrons in total. Step 2: octet rule for N, duet rule for H. Step 3: 6 electrons (3 pairs) in 3 bonds between H and N. The duet rule for H is satisfied. 2 electrons left. Step 4: 2 non-bonding electrons give N 5 valence electrons and make the structure neutral.
9.q What does the Lewis structure of the ion NH<sub>2</sub><sup>−</sup> look like?
9.a N forms two bonds with H. The duet rule is satisfied for H. N satisfies the octet rule, with 4 electrons in the 2 covalent bonds and 4 non-bonding electrons. That gives 6 electrons for N, 1 electron more than the 5 valence electrons of N, which gives a formal negative charge on the N atom: [NH<sub>2</sub>]<sup>−</sup>
10.q How is the formal charge distributed in CO<sub>3</sub><sup>2−</sup>?
10.a C forms 4 bonds: 1 double bond with O and 2 single bonds with O. The doubly bonded O atom has 2 non-bonding electron pairs and so satisfies the octet rule. The 2 singly bonded O atoms each have 3 non-bonding electron pairs, so they satisfy the octet rule, and each of these O atoms has 1 electron more than its number of valence electrons and is negatively charged.
11.q What does VSEPR stand for?
11.a Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion
12.q What is the steric number of CO<sub>2</sub>, SO<sub>2</sub>, CF<sub>4</sub> and NH<sub>3</sub>?
13.q Give the shape of molecules with a steric number of 2, 3, 4
13.a Linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral
14.q Give the angles in degrees for molecules with a steric number of 2, 3, 4
14.a 180, 120, 109 degrees
15.q Explain what dipoles are
15.a Partial charges in a molecule
16.q Explain what the steric number is
16.a The sum of the number of atoms bonded directly to the central atom plus the number of non-bonding pairs
17.q Does CO<sub>2</sub> have a dipole?
17.a No. CO<sub>2</sub> has a linear structure, there is a partial negative charge on the oxygen atoms and a positive charge on the central carbon atom, but because of the symmetry the charges cancel each other out
18.q Does SO<sub>2</sub> have a dipole?
18.a Yes. According to VSEPR, SO<sub>2</sub> has a trigonal planar arrangement. There is a partial negative charge on the oxygen atoms and a positive charge on the central S atom. Because of the non-bonding electron pair on the S atom, the molecule has a bent shape, so the negative charges do not cancel each other out. This creates a dipole
19.q Why does SO<sub>2</sub> dissolve well in water and CO<sub>2</sub> does not?
19.a SO<sub>2</sub> has a dipole. Water also has a dipole, so a dipole–dipole interaction can form between the two molecules
20.q Explain what resonance is
20.a The movement of electrons within a molecule without the molecule changing chemically.
21.q How can you tell from the structural formula whether a substance shows resonance?
21.a If you can draw several Lewis structures of the same molecule, there is resonance
22.q What general property do resonance structures give a molecule?
22.a The more resonance structures a molecule has, the more stable the substance is.
23.q Benzene, C<sub>6</sub>H<sub>6</sub>, has 3 double bonds. Explain how resonance works.
23.a Benzene has 3 double and 3 single bonds: the electrons of the 3 double bonds can move continuously, so that the single bonds become double and the earlier double bonds become single.
24.q How are the different resonance structures of benzene shown in one structure?
24.a By drawing the double bond as a circle inside the honeycomb-shaped hexagon

49.q Why do many base metals not corrode away completely?
49.a They react in such a way that a metal oxide or metal hydroxide layer forms that is impermeable, so the corrosion stops
50.q Why does iron rust away completely?
50.a A porous oxide/hydroxide layer forms, so the corrosion continues and the rust flakes off
51.q Which two kinds of protection of metals against corrosion exist?
51.a Passive and active
52.q Name an active method of protecting a metal
52.a Galvanising. Applying a layer of another metal.
53.q Name a passive method of protecting a metal
53.a Coating or painting with lacquer or plastic
54.q Name two ways of galvanising.
54.a Dipping in a liquid metal; applying a layer of metal electrochemically
55.q Can you galvanise with a more reactive metal?
55.a Yes, for example a zinc layer on iron. Zinc itself forms a thin layer of zinc oxide that protects both the zinc underneath and the iron
56.q How do noble, base and very reactive metals differ in chemical/redox terms?
56.a The stronger a reducing agent the metal is, the more reactive (less noble) it is
57.q In salt water corrosion goes faster because it conducts current; how can we protect steel ships against corrosion? Explain.
57.a By attaching sacrificial metal: zinc blocks fitted to the steel hull; because iron is slightly more noble than zinc, the zinc will corrode away first and only then the iron
58.q What is an electrochemical cell? Explain.
58.a A set-up in which the oxidation and reduction reactions take place separately, so that electrons can flow through a wire to power electrical devices, e.g. a lamp
59.q Which metals are used in a Daniell cell? What is the reducing agent and what is the oxidising agent?
59.a Copper, the oxidising agent is Cu<sup>2+</sup>; zinc, the reducing agent is Zn (s)
60.q Which metal seems to disappear in a Daniell cell and which one grows?
60.a Zinc seems to disappear, it dissolves as Zn<sup>2+</sup>; copper seems to appear out of nowhere
61.q Which metal is the + and which the − electrode in a Daniell cell?
61.a Zinc is the − electrode, it donates electrons; copper is the + electrode, it passes the electrons on to the copper ions in the solution
62.q What is a salt bridge and why is it needed in an electrochemical cell?
62.a A salt bridge is a connection between the reducing-agent and the oxidising-agent solutions, needed to balance the flow of electrons so that the total charge transfer is zero. E.g. a gel with KCl or NaCl
63.q Are there inert electrodes? What are they, and if so, do you know one?
63.a Yes, for example platinum or carbon electrodes; they do not take part in the chemical reaction that occurs, they only conduct the electric current
